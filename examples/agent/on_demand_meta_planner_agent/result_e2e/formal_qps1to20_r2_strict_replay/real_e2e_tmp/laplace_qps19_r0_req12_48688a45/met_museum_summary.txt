# The Metropolitan Museum of Art: History, Significance, and Main Collections

## History
The Metropolitan Museum of Art (the Met) was founded in 1870 when the New York State Legislature granted it an Act of Incorporation "for the purpose of establishing and maintaining in said City a Museum and Library of Art, of encouraging and developing the Study of the Fine Arts." The museum was established by a group of Americans including philanthropists, artists, and businessmen who wanted to create a national institution that would inspire and educate the public.

Key historical milestones:
- April 13, 1870: Official incorporation
- February 20, 1872: First opened to the public at 681 Fifth Avenue
- 1873: Moved to the Mrs. Nicholas Cruger Mansion at 128 West 14th Street
- 1880: Moved to its current location at 1000 Fifth Avenue
- 1938: The Cloisters, a separate building dedicated to medieval art, opened in Fort Tryon Park

Notable founders included John Taylor Johnston (first president), George Palmer Putnam (founding superintendent), Eastman Johnson, Frederic Edwin Church, Howard Potter, Salem Howe Wales, and Henry Gurdon Marquand. Luigi Palma di Cesnola served as the first director from 1879 to 1904.

## Significance
The Met is one of the world's most important cultural institutions:
- By floor area, it is the fourth-largest museum in the world and the largest art museum in the Americas
- It houses approximately 1.5 million works of art (down from over 2 million in 2000)
- The collection is divided into 17 curatorial departments
- The main building spans more than 2 million square feet (190,000 m²)
- In fiscal year 2025, it had 5,727,258 visitors, making it the most-visited museum in the United States and the fifth-most visited art museum globally
- It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1986

## Main Collections
The Met's encyclopedic collection spans 5,000 years of world culture and includes:

### Geographically Designated Collections
1. **Ancient Near Eastern Art**: Over 7,000 pieces from the Neolithic Period through the fall of the Sasanian Empire, including works from Sumerian, Hittite, Assyrian, Babylonian, and Elamite cultures.

2. **Arts of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas**: More than 11,000 pieces housed in the Michael C. Rockefeller Wing, featuring works from sub-Saharan Africa, the Pacific Islands, and the Americas dating from 3,000 BCE to the present.

3. **Asian Art**: Over 35,000 pieces spanning 4,000 years, with comprehensive collections of Cambodian, Indian, Chinese, Nepalese, Tibetan, Burmese, and Thai art.

4. **Egyptian Art**: More than 26,000 objects from the Paleolithic era through the Ptolemaic era, including the famous Temple of Dendur and wooden models from Middle Kingdom tombs.

5. **European Paintings**: Over 2,600 works from the 13th through early 20th centuries, with strengths in Old Masters, Impressionist, and Post-Impressionist paintings.

6. **Greek and Roman Art**: More than 17,000 objects, including the museum's first accessioned object—a Roman sarcophagus—and highlights like the Monteleone chariot.

7. **Islamic Art**: One of the world's largest collections with 12,000 objects from Spain to Central Asia, including miniature paintings, ceramics, textiles, and calligraphy.

### Non-Geographically Designated Collections
1. **American Wing**: Art from the 18th through early 20th century, including the first display of Indigenous American art in 2018.

2. **Arms and Armor**: 14,000 objects spanning cultures from prehistoric times to the 20th century, including armor belonging to European royalty.

3. **Costume Institute**: Over 35,000 costumes and accessories, known for hosting the annual Met Gala.

4. **Drawings and Prints**: About 21,000 drawings, 1.2 million prints, and 12,000 illustrated books.

5. **Robert Lehman Collection**: 2,600 works donated in 1969, described as "one of the most extraordinary private art collections ever assembled in the United States."

6. **Medieval Art and The Cloisters**: Over 10,000 objects, with about 6,000 in the main building and 5,000 at The Cloisters, including the famous Hunt of the Unicorn tapestries.

7. **Modern and Contemporary Art**: 13,000 artworks occupying 60,000 square feet of gallery space, including iconic works by Picasso, Pollock, and Johns.

8. **Musical Instruments**: About 5,000 instruments from around the world, including Stradivari violins and the oldest surviving piano (1720).

9. **Photographs**: More than 25,000 photographs from major collections including those of Alfred Stieglitz and Walker Evans.

The Met continues to evolve, with plans to merge with the Neue Galerie New York in 2028, adding its collection of early 20th-century Austrian and German art to the Met's holdings.
