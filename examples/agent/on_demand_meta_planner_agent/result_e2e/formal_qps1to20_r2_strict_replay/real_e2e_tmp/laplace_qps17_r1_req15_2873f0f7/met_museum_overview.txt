# The Metropolitan Museum of Art: Overview

## History
The Metropolitan Museum of Art (the Met) was founded in 1870 by a group of American philanthropists, artists, and businessmen with the goal of creating a national institution to inspire and educate the public. The New York State Legislature granted the museum an Act of Incorporation on April 13, 1870. The museum first opened on February 20, 1872, at 681 Fifth Avenue, before moving to its current location on Fifth Avenue along Central Park's eastern edge in 1880. Key founders included railroad executive John Taylor Johnston (first president), publisher George Palmer Putnam (founding superintendent), and artists Eastman Johnson and Frederic Edwin Church. The museum's first director was Luigi Palma di Cesnola, who served from 1879 to 1904.

## Significance
- By floor area, the Met is the fourth-largest museum in the world and the largest art museum in the Americas
- It houses approximately 1.5 million works of art (down from over 2 million in 2000)
- The collection is organized into 17 curatorial departments
- With over 5.7 million visitors annually (as of fiscal year 2025), it is the most-visited museum in the United States and the fifth-most visited art museum globally
- The main building spans approximately 2 million square feet, making it one of the world's largest art museums
- The museum operates two additional locations: The Cloisters (medieval European art) and will merge with the Neue Galerie in 2028 (early 20th century Austrian and German art)

## Main Collections

### Geographically Designated Collections
- **Ancient Near Eastern Art**: Over 7,000 pieces spanning from the Neolithic Period through Late Antiquity, including Sumerian, Hittite, Assyrian, Babylonian, and Elamite works
- **Egyptian Art**: More than 26,000 objects from the Paleolithic era through the Ptolemaic era, featuring the iconic Temple of Dendur as its centerpiece
- **Greek and Roman Art**: Over 17,000 objects including the museum's first accessioned piece (a Roman sarcophagus), Cycladic sculptures, and the Monteleone chariot
- **Asian Art**: More than 35,000 pieces spanning 4,000 years, with comprehensive collections of Chinese, Indian, Cambodian, and Japanese art
- **Islamic Art**: 12,000 works from Spain to Central Asia, including miniature paintings, calligraphy, and the reconstructed Nur Al-Din Room from Damascus
- **Arts of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas**: Over 11,000 pieces housed in the Michael C. Rockefeller Wing, ranging from 40,000-year-old Australian rock paintings to Benin Court artifacts

### Non-Geographically Designated Collections
- **European Paintings**: Over 2,600 works from the 13th through early 20th centuries, with strengths in Old Masters, Impressionist, and Post-Impressionist art
- **American Wing**: Comprehensive collection of American art from the 18th through early 20th centuries, including Indigenous American art
- **Modern and Contemporary Art**: Approximately 13,000 works including iconic pieces by Picasso, Pollock, and Johns
- **Arms and Armor**: 14,000 objects spanning global cultures from prehistoric times to the 20th century
- **Costume Institute**: Over 35,000 costumes and accessories, featured in special exhibitions and the annual Met Gala
- **Musical Instruments**: About 5,000 instruments from around the world, including Stradivari violins and the oldest surviving piano (1720)
- **Drawings and Prints**: 21,000 drawings, 1.2 million prints, and 12,000 illustrated books
- **Photographs**: Over 25,000 photographs from major collections including Alfred Stieglitz and Walker Evans
- **Robert Lehman Collection**: 2,600 works donated in 1969, considered "one of the most extraordinary private art collections ever assembled in the United States"

### The Cloisters
A separate building in Fort Tryon Park dedicated solely to medieval European art, featuring the Hunt of the Unicorn tapestries, the Belles Heures manuscript, and architectural elements from five French monasteries.
