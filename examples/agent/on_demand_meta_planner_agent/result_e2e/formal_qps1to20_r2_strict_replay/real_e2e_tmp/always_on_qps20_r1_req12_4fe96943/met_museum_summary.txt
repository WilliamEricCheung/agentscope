# The Metropolitan Museum of Art: History, Significance, and Collections

## History
The Metropolitan Museum of Art (the Met) was founded in 1870 by a group of Americans including philanthropists, artists, and businessmen with the goal of creating a national institution that would inspire and educate the public. The New York State Legislature granted the museum an Act of Incorporation on April 13, 1870.

The museum first opened on February 20, 1872, at 681 Fifth Avenue. Its initial collection consisted of a Roman stone sarcophagus and 174 mostly European paintings. John Taylor Johnston, a railroad executive whose personal art collection seeded the museum, served as its first president. The artist Eastman Johnson and landscape painter Frederic Edwin Church were among the co-founders.

In 1873, the museum moved to the Douglas Mansion at 128 West 14th Street after acquiring the Cesnola Collection of Cypriot antiquities. By 1880, the growing collection required more space, leading to the move to its current location at 1000 Fifth Avenue along Museum Mile on the eastern edge of Central Park.

The first portion of the approximately 2-million-square-foot building was constructed in 1880. Over time, the museum has expanded significantly through additions and renovations, becoming an accretion of over 20 structures. A second location, The Cloisters at Fort Tryon Park in Upper Manhattan, was completed in 1938 and houses the museum's medieval European collection. A third site, the Neue Galerie New York, is set to become the museum's newest location in 2028 when the Met merges with and absorbs this smaller museum dedicated to early 20th century Austrian and German art.

## Significance
The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds tremendous cultural and historical significance:

- It is the fourth-largest museum in the world by floor area and the largest art museum in the Americas
- With 5.7 million visitors in fiscal year 2025, it was the most-visited museum in the United States and the fifth-most visited art museum globally
- The museum houses approximately 1.5 million works of art (down from over 2 million in 2000)
- Its collection is organized into 17 curatorial departments with specialized staff
- The Met serves as a cultural landmark in New York City and a major educational resource
- The main building was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1986

## Main Collections
The Met's encyclopedic collection spans 5,000 years of world culture and includes:

### Geographically Designated Collections
1. **Ancient Near Eastern Art**: Over 7,000 pieces representing cultures from the Neolithic Period through Late Antiquity, including Sumerian, Hittite, Assyrian, Babylonian, and Elamite works.

2. **Arts of Africa, Oceania, and the Americas**: More than 11,000 pieces housed in the Michael C. Rockefeller Wing, featuring works from sub-Saharan Africa, the Pacific Islands, and the Americas dating from 40,000 BCE to present.

3. **Asian Art**: Over 35,000 pieces spanning 4,000 years, with comprehensive collections of Chinese, Indian, Cambodian, Nepalese, Tibetan, Burmese, and Thai art. The Asian wing includes the Astor Court, a complete Ming Dynasty-style garden court.

4. **Egyptian Art**: More than 26,000 objects from the Paleolithic era through the Ptolemaic era displayed in 40 Egyptian galleries. Highlights include the Temple of Dendur, wooden models from the Middle Kingdom, and William the Faience Hippopotamus (the museum's informal mascot).

5. **European Paintings**: Over 2,600 works from the 13th through early 20th centuries, including significant collections of Old Master paintings, Impressionist, and Post-Impressionist works. The collection features works by masters such as Rembrandt, Vermeer, Goya, Monet, Van Gogh, and Cézanne.

6. **Greek and Roman Art**: More than 17,000 objects including the museum's first accessioned object—a Roman sarcophagus—still on display. Highlights include the Monteleone chariot and Cycladic sculptures.

7. **Islamic Art**: One of the world's largest collections of Islamic art with approximately 12,000 objects spanning from Spain to Central Asia, including miniature paintings, ceramics, textiles, and the reconstructed Nur Al-Din Room from Damascus.

### Non-Geographically Designated Collections
1. **American Wing**: Comprehensive collection of American art from the 18th through early 20th centuries, including the 2018 addition of Indigenous American art.

2. **Arms and Armor**: One of the museum's most popular collections with 14,000 objects spanning cultures from prehistoric times to the 20th century, including armor belonging to European royalty.

3. **Costume Institute**: Over 35,000 costumes and accessories, known for hosting the annual Met Gala and thematic exhibitions like "Alexander McQueen: Savage Beauty."

4. **Drawings and Prints**: Approximately 21,000 drawings, 1.2 million prints, and 12,000 illustrated books from Europe and the Americas.

5. **Robert Lehman Collection**: 2,600 works donated in 1969, considered "one of the most extraordinary private art collections ever assembled in the United States," with strengths in Italian Renaissance painting.

6. **Medieval Art and The Cloisters**: Over 10,000 objects of Western art from the 4th through early 16th centuries, with highlights including the Belles Heures manuscript and the Hunt of the Unicorn tapestries.

7. **Modern and Contemporary Art**: 13,000 artworks occupying 60,000 square feet of gallery space, including iconic works by Picasso, Pollock, Johns, and Beckmann, plus a significant Cubist collection donated by Leonard Lauder.

8. **Musical Instruments**: Approximately 5,000 instruments from around the world, including Stradivari violins and the oldest surviving piano (1720).

9. **Photographs**: Over 25,000 photographs from five major collections, including works by Stieglitz, Steichen, and Walker Evans.

The Met continues to evolve as both a repository of global artistic heritage and a dynamic cultural institution engaged with contemporary issues of provenance, representation, and accessibility.
